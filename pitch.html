Weekly goals · Reflection · Recognition

Momentum is a workplace app for weekly goals, reflection and recognition. The tagline comes straight from the app's own "Why This App?" page.

In one sentence: Momentum helps people experience progress, find meaning in their everyday work and feel connected to the people they work with.

Over the next slides I'll show the problem it solves, the idea and science behind it, and then walk through the working prototype from three perspectives: employee, leader and CEO.

The problem

Motivation isn't given. It's created by conditions.

Goal tools tend to drift into surveillance and pressure. People end up ticking boxes instead of growing.

What goal tools often become
Monitoring Comparing Ranking

Most goal tools start with good intentions, but they easily slide into monitoring, micromanaging and comparing people. Then people optimise for what is measured: they tick boxes instead of developing.

The headline is the core belief of the Leader Handbook, chapter 1: "Motivation is not something you simply give to people. It is something you can create the conditions for."

Chapter 3 names the trap directly: if attention goes only to completed goals, people "may gradually learn that ticking boxes matters more than experimenting, learning or helping others."

So the design question for Momentum was: how do we build the conditions for motivation, rather than another place to prove performance?

Built on Autonomy · Competence · Relatedness + Mastery-oriented climate + WOOP

Momentum in one line: a small, supportive part of the working week that helps people experience progress, find meaning and feel connected.

Three pillars: Progress (two weekly goals plus one monthly goal, chosen by the employee), Reflection (a short Friday reflection in their own words) and Connection (Bravo! to cheer colleagues on, and monthly department and CEO updates).

The science underneath:

  • Self-determination theory: three basic psychological needs, autonomy, competence and relatedness. The Leader Handbook chapter 2 is built on them, and the AI summary codes reflections against them.
  • A mastery-oriented climate (Handbook chapter 3): success is learning, effort, improvement and collaboration, not outperforming others.
  • WOOP (Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan): a science-based mental strategy used for the monthly goal. More at woopmylife.org.

The rhythm is taken from the app's "How to Use Momentum" page:

  • Start of the month: one monthly goal for broader direction, worked through with WOOP.
  • Start of the week: two weekly goals that reflect your own priorities. They can support the monthly goal, or not.
  • Throughout the week: mark goals as achieved, adjust them if priorities change, and send a Bravo! when you feel like it.
  • End of the week: a Friday reflection on what you learned, what helped and what got in the way.
  • Once a month: read updates from other departments and the CEO.

The app says it plainly: it is "intended to be a small and supportive part of your working week, not another task on your to-do list." Set aside time at the start and end of the week; the rest is optional.

Monthly goal + WOOP

Ambition, with a plan for what stops you.

WOOP is a science-based strategy: name the inner obstacle, then make an if–then plan.

Once a month, Kari sets one more ambitious goal: something she wants to learn or get better at. "No numbers or targets needed."

Her real WOOP from the prototype:

  • Wish: Get comfortable leading customer interviews on my own.
  • Outcome: I lead interviews calmly, customers open up, and I feel I really understand what they need, which is why I do this job.
  • Obstacle: I get nervous and over-prepare questions instead of practising out loud.
  • Plan: If I notice I'm rewriting my interview guide the day before, then I'll do a ten-minute practice interview with a colleague instead.

The obstacle is deliberately an inner one: "A habit, feeling or thought – not something outside you." The app frames it as preparation, not weakness: "Recognizing an obstacle is not a sign of weakness, but an opportunity to prepare for it." Obstacles outside your control are for the Friday reflection and your manager.

Notice how her weekly goal 1 (interview with Silje observing) is a stepping stone toward this wish.

At the end of the week, Kari writes a short reflection. The prompt in the app reads: "What did you learn? Where did you get to decide for yourself? Who helped you – or who did you help? What got in the way? Unfinished goals are useful information, not failure."

The four questions line up with the three psychological needs (competence, autonomy, relatedness) plus obstacles. The tags on the slide show that mapping; they are not shown in the app itself. Those are exactly the themes the leader's summary later codes.

From the FAQ: "There is no right or wrong answer." And: "Not achieving a goal is a natural part of working life and does not have negative consequences in Momentum."

Privacy: only Kari and her manager can read it.

From the app's "Privacy – Who Sees What" page:

  • Goals: the content of your monthly and weekly goals is only visible to you and your manager. Colleagues see when you achieve a goal, but not what it is about.
  • WOOP and Friday reflection: only you and your manager.
  • Monthly updates: department and CEO updates are visible across the organization, and focus on collective contributions, not individual performance.
  • CEO: sees aggregated data per department. "Individual goals and reflections are visible only to the department's leader."

The promise: "Momentum is not a tool for monitoring, micromanaging or evaluating your performance. Your goals, reflections and level of engagement with Momentum are not to be used for performance evaluations, employee rankings or compensation decisions."

This is the single biggest difference from typical goal tools.

Once a month every department leader writes an update, and the CEO writes one that ties them together. They are visible to everyone and focus on collective learning and contributions, not individual performance.

The writing prompt in the app nudges the tone: "Describe progress compared to where you were, not compared to other teams… Avoid rankings, targets hit/missed or 'best/top' language." Updates must not include anything from personal goals or reflections.

Hanne's November update connects the four department stories: Product understood customers, Design made it intuitive, Engineering built the foundation, People & Culture improved the conditions for everyone. Her line: "These are not four separate stories. They are different parts of the same story."

This is how Momentum connects everyday work to a bigger picture, and colleagues can send a Bravo! to a whole department.

The example is from chapter 4. Kari writes that she didn't manage the interview on her own, that she over-prepared because she was nervous, and that having Silje observe helped. Note how it echoes her WOOP obstacle.

The less supportive response puts pressure on her. The more supportive one acknowledges her perspective, recognizes learning and offers support (the full version also offers time for a practice interview) "while preserving ownership of the next step."

The point: Momentum doesn't just give leaders data; it teaches them how to respond to it.

To sum up the story: goal tools often become surveillance. Momentum starts from the opposite belief, that motivation is created by conditions.

  • For employees: small goals they own, a WOOP plan, and a Friday reflection where unfinished goals are information, not failure.
  • For colleagues: recognition without exposure. See that, never what.
  • For leaders: signals to support, not scores to judge, plus a handbook on how to respond.
  • For the CEO: the big picture in aggregate, and one shared story every month.

The closing line is the last line of both the Leader Handbook and Hanne's CEO update: "Our momentum is something we create together."
